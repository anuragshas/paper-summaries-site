Thesis

LLM agents, when guided by a formal framework for combinatorial experiment search, perform genuine architecture discovery rather than mere hyperparameter tuning, with architectural choices explaining the dominant portion of performance variance. Their search dynamics follow predictable power-law convergence patterns, enabling the identification of qualitatively better architectural regions compared to random or Bayesian baselines through systematic exploration-exploitation cycles and emergent specialization.

Core Problem

When LLM agents autonomously design ML experiments, do they perform genuine architecture search—or do they default to hyperparameter tuning within a narrow region of the design space?

Key Novelty

Formalized empirical framework and convergence analysis for LLM-guided combinatorial ML experiment search, quantifying architectural vs. hyperparameter contributions and multi-agent dynamics.

Key Contributions

  • Architectural choices (backbone \times encoder) explain 94% of performance variance on the Nexar dataset and 75% on the FedEx dataset, confirming genuine architecture discovery.
  • LLM-guided search achieves 0.9852 AP at N=50 experiments, outperforming a from-scratch random baseline (0.9648 AP) and converging to a higher asymptote (0.994 AP vs. 0.976 AP for random).
  • The power-law convergence exponent for LLM-guided search is c=0.11, which is lower than pool-based random (0.99) and TPE (1.12) baselines, reflecting the cost of broad exploration that leads to qualitatively better architectural regions.
  • Multi-agent dynamics show exploration-exploitation cycles in configuration entropy and emergent specialization, with agents converging on the winning VJepa2 backbone, demonstrating adaptive behavior.

Results

  • LLM agents perform genuine architecture search, with architectural choices (backbone and encoder) explaining the vast majority (94%) of performance variance, rather than merely tuning hyperparameters.
  • The LLM-guided search process follows a power-law convergence model, with a convergence exponent of c=0.11, demonstrating the cost of broad architectural exploration leading to higher asymptotic performance compared to random baselines.
  • The multi-agent system exhibits dynamic exploration-exploitation cycles, emergent specialization (e.g., convergence on VJepa2 backbone), and a power-law decline in innovation rate.
  • The framework discovers task-specific winning architectures (e.g., VJepa2 for Nexar, SigLIP2 for FedEx), demonstrating adaptability beyond memorizing a single configuration.

Limitations

  • The architecture-dominance finding is validated on two collision detection tasks, but convergence exponents are measured on only one; replication on diverse task types (NLP, tabular) is needed for broader generalization.
  • The convergence hypothesis is empirical, not a formal theorem, and proving regret bounds would require assumptions about LLM generalization that current tools cannot formalize.
  • Pool-based baselines (random, TPE) inherit the LLM's initial selection bias. The from-scratch random baseline is relatively small (172 configs) compared to the 108K-cell space.
  • The LLM search policy is non-reproducible, and reported statistics are aggregate. The LLM explored only 2.4% of the discrete configuration space.
  • A 'configuration verification gap' exists where LLM agents operate at the specification layer but cannot verify code execution, leading to silent misconfigurations that were resolved by human debugging.
